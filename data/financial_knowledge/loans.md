# Loans and debt

A loan is borrowed money repaid over time with interest. The two questions that
matter most are what happens if repayment fails, and how the interest is
calculated.

## Secured versus unsecured

A **secured loan** is backed by an asset — the collateral. Home loans are
secured against the property, vehicle loans against the vehicle, gold loans
against pledged gold. Because the lender can recover the asset on default, risk
is lower and interest rates are correspondingly lower. The consequence of
default is loss of the asset.

An **unsecured loan** has no collateral. Personal loans, credit cards and most
education loans fall here. Approval depends on income and credit history rather
than an asset, so rates are higher. Default does not cost a specific asset but
damages credit standing and can lead to recovery proceedings.

As a rough ordering of cost, from cheapest to most expensive: home loan, then
vehicle loan, then education loan, then personal loan, then credit card
borrowing.

## EMI

An EMI, or equated monthly instalment, is a fixed monthly payment covering both
interest and principal so the loan is fully repaid by the end of its term.

The payment stays constant, but its composition changes. Early instalments are
mostly interest, because interest is charged on a large outstanding balance.
Later instalments are mostly principal. This is why repaying early in the term
saves far more interest than repaying the same amount near the end, and why the
total interest paid over a long loan can be a substantial fraction of the amount
borrowed.

Extending the tenure lowers the monthly payment and raises the total interest —
often considerably. A longer loan is not a cheaper loan.

## Fixed and floating rates

A **fixed rate** stays the same for the term, or an agreed part of it. Payments
are predictable, usually at a slightly higher starting rate.

A **floating rate** moves with a benchmark. Payments can fall, and can equally
rise. Lenders often absorb changes by extending the tenure rather than raising
the EMI, which keeps the monthly figure stable while increasing total interest.

## Debt-to-income

Lenders compare total monthly debt payments with monthly income. A ratio below
about 36%, with housing under roughly 28%, is a common comfort threshold.
Beyond that, borrowing is generally harder and more expensive, and the budget
has little room to absorb a shock.

## Order of repayment

With multiple debts, two approaches are widely used:

- **Avalanche.** Pay the highest interest rate first. Mathematically optimal —
  it minimises total interest paid.
- **Snowball.** Pay the smallest balance first. Costs slightly more overall but
  clears individual debts sooner, which some people find easier to sustain.

Either way, minimum payments continue on everything else. The avalanche saves
more money; the snowball is sometimes more likely to be followed to completion.

## Refinancing

Replacing a loan with a cheaper one can reduce cost, but processing fees,
prepayment penalties and a reset tenure can erase the benefit. The comparison
that matters is total remaining cost under each option, not the interest rate
alone.
