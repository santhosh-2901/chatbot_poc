# Budgeting

A budget is a plan for money before it is spent. Its purpose is not restriction
but visibility: deciding where money should go in advance, rather than
discovering afterwards where it went.

## The 50/30/20 rule

The 50/30/20 rule splits take-home income into three parts:

- **50% needs.** Housing, utilities, groceries, transport to work, insurance,
  minimum debt repayments. Things that continue whether or not you want them to.
- **30% wants.** Eating out, shopping, subscriptions, entertainment, travel.
  Spending that improves life but could be paused.
- **20% savings and debt repayment.** Building an emergency fund, investing,
  and paying down debt faster than the minimum.

It is popular because it is easy to remember and needs no categories beyond
three. It is a starting point, not a law. In cities with high rent, needs alone
can exceed 50%, and forcing the split can mean pretending an essential cost is
optional. The useful discipline is protecting the 20% first, then letting needs
and wants divide what remains.

A common mistake is applying the percentages to gross salary. They apply to
take-home pay, after tax and mandatory deductions.

## Zero-based budgeting

Every unit of income is assigned a job until nothing is unallocated: income
minus all allocations equals zero. Savings and investments are allocations, not
leftovers. This is more work than 50/30/20 but eliminates the vague surplus that
tends to be spent by default.

## Envelope budgeting

Each category gets a fixed allowance for the month. When the allowance is spent,
spending in that category stops until the next month. Originally literal cash in
envelopes, now usually separate accounts or app categories. Effective for
categories with many small discretionary purchases, where a total is easy to
lose track of.

## Paying yourself first

Savings are transferred out on payday, before discretionary spending begins,
rather than being whatever survives the month. This reverses the usual order and
is one of the most reliable ways to raise a savings rate, because it removes the
need for month-long willpower.

## Building a budget from history

A budget invented from nothing usually fails, because it reflects intention
rather than behaviour. A more durable approach starts from what actually
happened over recent months:

1. Total each category over the last three to six months.
2. Take the **median** rather than the average. One unusual month drags an
   average upward, and budgeting to that inflated figure defeats the purpose.
3. Fund essentials at that level, since they are largely fixed.
4. Set a savings target before allocating discretionary spending.
5. Fit discretionary categories into what remains.

Fixed costs such as rent need no headroom — they are the same every month, and
padding them just hides money that could be saved.

## Why budgets fail

- Built on gross rather than take-home income.
- No allowance for irregular annual costs like insurance premiums.
- Too many categories to track.
- No buffer, so a single unexpected expense makes the whole plan void.
- Treating savings as a remainder rather than an allocation.
