# Investing basics

Investing means committing money to assets expected to grow over time, accepting
that value can fall in the short term. It is distinct from saving, where the
priority is that the money is intact when needed.

This is general education. Nothing here is a recommendation to buy or sell any
specific investment.

## Compound interest

Compound interest is interest earned on both the original amount and on the
interest already accumulated. Growth accelerates, because each period starts
from a larger base.

    future value = principal x (1 + rate) ^ periods

10,000 at 8% a year becomes about 21,600 after ten years and about 46,600 after
twenty. The first decade adds around 11,600; the second adds around 25,000 from
the same starting deposit. Nothing changed except elapsed time.

This is why starting early matters more than starting large. Someone investing
5,000 a month from age 25 typically ends up ahead of someone investing 10,000 a
month from age 40, despite contributing less in total.

Compounding works in reverse on debt. Credit card interest compounds against the
borrower with the same mathematics.

## The rule of 72

Dividing 72 by the annual return gives the approximate years to double. At 8%,
roughly nine years. At 12%, roughly six. Useful for quick mental comparison, not
for precision.

## Risk and return

Higher expected returns come with greater variability. That variability is the
cost of the return, not a flaw to be engineered away.

- **Low risk** — savings accounts, fixed deposits, government securities.
  Modest, predictable returns.
- **Moderate risk** — debt funds, balanced funds, corporate bonds.
- **Higher risk** — equities and equity funds. Historically the strongest
  long-term returns, with large short-term falls along the way.

The relevant question is not which is best but which matches the time horizon.
Money needed in two years does not belong in equities, regardless of expected
return, because the horizon offers no time to recover from a fall.

## Diversification

Spreading money across assets that do not move together reduces the impact of
any single one performing badly. Holding ten stocks in the same industry is far
less diversified than it appears, because they tend to fall together.

Diversification reduces the risk specific to one company or sector. It does not
remove market-wide risk.

## Inflation

Inflation erodes purchasing power. At 6% inflation, money held in cash loses
roughly half its real value over twelve years. A savings account paying 3% while
inflation runs at 6% is losing about 3% a year in real terms.

This is the argument for investing money that is not needed soon — and equally
the argument for keeping an emergency fund in cash anyway, because certainty of
value matters more than real return for money that must be available.

## Sequence

A widely used ordering, before investing begins:

1. Cover essential expenses.
2. Build an emergency fund of three to six months of essentials.
3. Clear high-interest debt, particularly credit cards.
4. Then invest with a horizon of five years or more.

Investing while carrying credit card debt at 36% is usually a losing trade: few
investments reliably return more than that, and the debt's cost is certain while
the return is not.
