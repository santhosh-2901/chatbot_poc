# Saving and savings rate

Saving is the gap between income and spending. Everything else — where to put
the money, what return it earns — matters less than the size of that gap,
especially early on.

## Savings rate

The savings rate is the share of take-home income not spent:

    savings rate = (income - expenses) / income

Someone earning 60,000 and spending 42,500 has a savings rate of about 29%.

It is a more useful measure than the absolute amount saved, because it adjusts
for income. Rising income with an unchanged savings rate means spending grew in
step — the pattern usually called lifestyle inflation.

## Why the rate matters more than the return

Early on, the amount saved dominates the return earned. Saving an extra 5,000 a
month adds 60,000 a year with certainty, while chasing two extra percentage
points of return on a 200,000 portfolio adds 4,000 and is not guaranteed. Only
once the invested amount is large does return begin to dominate. This is why
advice tends to emphasise the savings rate first and portfolio construction
second.

## Paying yourself first

Transferring savings on payday, before discretionary spending, is more reliable
than saving what remains at month end. It converts a month of daily decisions
into one automated transfer.

## Sinking funds

Not every large expense is an emergency. Insurance premiums, festival spending,
annual subscriptions and travel are predictable — they are simply not monthly.
A sinking fund sets aside a twelfth of the annual cost each month, so the
expense arrives already funded. This is what stops predictable costs from
draining the emergency fund.

An annual premium of 18,000 becomes a monthly allocation of 1,500.

## Where savings go, by time horizon

- **Under a year** — savings account or liquid fund. Capital stability matters
  more than return.
- **One to five years** — fixed deposits, recurring deposits, short-duration
  debt funds. Some return, limited volatility.
- **Over five years** — long-term instruments including equity, where time
  smooths out volatility.

Matching the instrument to when the money is needed matters more than maximising
return. Money required next year does not belong anywhere its value can fall.

## Reducing spending without tracking every rupee

The largest savings usually come from a few recurring decisions rather than many
small ones. Rent, vehicle costs, and subscriptions are decided once and then
repeat automatically every month. Reviewing those few commitments is generally
worth more than scrutinising daily purchases.
